Executive Summary

A proven, working formula that is currently under-capitalized

Accessnetng sells prepaid WiFi access — daily, weekly and monthly vouchers — to students and residents in Malete, Kwara State, through an automated billing system ("Centipid") that logs a user in the moment they pay and logs them off the moment their time runs out, with no staff sitting at a desk manually managing access. The first attempt at this business, funded by my mother, failed and closed. I rebuilt it in 2026 with a different method, and in the two months since restarting, active users grew from 13 to 140+ across roughly 30 hostels, on 28 active routers, with zero outstanding debt to any equipment vendor. This document lays out exactly how that happened, what it costs to run today, and what a partnership investment would fund next.

How It Works

Automated voucher billing — no manual gatekeeping

Users connect to the "accessnetng" WiFi network and pay for a voucher. The Centipid billing system automatically authenticates the user for exactly the period paid for, then logs them off the moment it expires — no staff manually granting or revoking access, no trust-based tabs, no under-the-table sharing of a single login across dozens of people.

The Journey

Told straight — the failure is part of the proof

I started this business at 18. I am not going to present it as a straight line up, because it was not one. The reason I believe the current formula works is precisely because I have already run the version that didn't.

Phase 1 — First attempt, downtown Malete

Starting capital: ~₦1.8M–2M, funded by my mother

Roughly ₦1,000,000 covered a Starlink kit, a MikroTik router and 2 access points; ₦800,000 went to a solar setup; ₦200,000 to transportation. I had no one to teach me — I became my own teacher and pushed through as a first-time business owner still figuring out the basics.

Phase 2 — Moved to a single 80-room hostel, went into debt

Flat ₦5,000/month unlimited — priced below what it cost to run

Starlink alone was ₦159,000/month at the time. Customers weren't converting fast enough to cover it, and the routers pictured in our records were bought on vendor credit — debt I was carrying while running at a loss. When students went home for the semester break, demand collapsed and the business closed. It was not sustainable at that point, and I will not pretend otherwise.

Phase 3 — 2026 restart, back downtown

Better solar, a new method, and incremental funding

I resumed in 2026, moved back downtown, and put in new batteries (one funded by my mother, one I paid for myself). Instead of over-extending on day one, I scaled in small, funded steps: a ₦250,000 loan financed 5 additional routers, then 5 more after that, building up to 28 active routers covering roughly 30 hostels — this time with no vendor debt. That method is what took us from 13 to 140+ users in two months and to consistent monthly revenue in the ₦1.9M–₦3.2M range.

Marginal router payback: under 2 weeks. The ₦250,000 loan that funded the last 10 routers works out to ≈ ₦25,000 all-in per router (hardware + cabling). This math holds for routers added within the current network's existing bandwidth. Reaching entirely new, distant hostel clusters may eventually need added backbone capacity — a scaling cost not included above.

The Problem This Investment Solves

Unsteady solar is the single biggest cost leak

₦10,000/day in fuel (≈ ₦300,000/month, ≈ ₦3.65M/year) is currently being burned on generator backup because the existing solar setup is not steady enough to run the network without it. This is the largest single cost item in the business — larger than the Starlink subscription itself.

A properly sized, steady solar power system removes most or all of this daily fuel dependency. Every naira it eliminates converts directly into margin, without needing a single new customer — and it removes the biggest operational risk (fuel availability and price volatility) from a business that already has a proven customer-acquisition formula.

A starting point for discussion, not a signed agreement:

  • Investor contributes ₦5,000,000.

  • Once the ₦5,000,000 is fully repaid, investor receives 30% of monthly net profit for a further 6 months — a return on top of, and aside from, the ₦5,000,000 principal.

Risks & Honest Disclosures

What could go wrong

  • Seasonal demand: when students go home for breaks, active users and revenue drop — this is what closed the business the first time.
  • Power dependency: until solar is funded and installed, the network still relies on fuel, which is a volatile daily cost.
  • Key-person risk: billing, maintenance and router deployment currently run through one operator (the founder).
  • No audited financials yet: every number in this document is founder-reported and self-tracked, not third-party verified — Moniepoint statements are available on request.
  • Not yet CAC-registered: formalization is funded by this raise, not already complete.
  • The 12-month outlook is a model, not a forecast guarantee: it assumes demand growth and backbone capacity keep pace.
